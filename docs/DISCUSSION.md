# Discussion

## Principal finding

Among NBA Draft Combine participants from 2000 through 2025, public-name
distinctiveness did not provide reliable evidence of incremental draft
information beyond conventional anthropometric and athletic measurements. The
baseline-plus-name model had slightly better point estimates for log loss,
Brier score, discrimination, and average precision, but every paired bootstrap
interval included no difference. Given-name surprisal had essentially no
adjusted association with selection. Surname surprisal had a nominal inverse
association, but it did not survive the prespecified correction for two
co-primary tests.

The appropriate interpretation is not that names have been proved irrelevant.
Rather, within this selected population and under these operational
definitions, the study did not detect a stable or practically meaningful
increment beyond the available Combine measurements. The magnitude of the
primary predictive difference—less than one-thousandth in log loss—is too small
and uncertain to justify using name distinctiveness in draft evaluation.

## Interpretation

The motivating impression that successful professional players often have
memorable names is compatible with salience and recall rather than selection.
A distinctive name may make an already successful player easier to remember
without making that player more likely to be drafted. This study cannot test
the cognitive mechanism directly, but its results give little support to the
stronger claim that name rarity contains robust draft information after
accounting for measured prospect characteristics.

The nominal surname result should be treated cautiously. Its confidence
interval narrowly excluded one before multiplicity correction, its adjusted
*p* value was 0.082, and its direction changed in the exact-birth-year subset.
The result could reflect chance, residual differences in geography or pathway,
or other factors correlated with U.S. surname frequency. It is not evidence
that a surname causes a team to select or reject a prospect.

The exact-birth-year analysis provides a separate methodological lesson.
Birth-year coverage was nearly complete for drafted participants but below half
for undrafted participants. Restricting the study to records with verified
dates therefore changes the comparison group in a way directly related to the
outcome. The reversal of the tiny predictive point estimate and the surname
coefficient in that subset illustrates how apparently more precise individual
matching can be less credible when source completeness is outcome-dependent.
The common Combine-generation window preserved the full comparison cohort and
was the more defensible primary construct.

## Practical implications

Teams and public analysts should not add name-rarity variables to prospect
models on the basis of these results. The small and uncertain test-set changes
would not support a scouting or personnel decision, and name-based variables
carry obvious risks of encoding language, nationality, and cultural
familiarity. The practical contribution is instead procedural: unusual
candidate predictors should be evaluated against a temporal baseline, with
their missingness and reference populations audited before interpretation.

The findings also argue for separating memorability from player evaluation.
Names could plausibly affect media attention, search behavior, or fan recall
even though they did not materially improve draft prediction here. Those are
different outcomes and would require separately frozen designs.

## Strengths

The study used a defined prospect denominator containing drafted and undrafted
players, rather than examining only athletes who reached the NBA. It covered 26
Combine classes, preserved repeat appearances, separated given names from
surnames, and encoded disclosure-threshold uncertainty conservatively. The
analysis plan was frozen before outcome modeling, the final four years were
held out temporally, and the locked comparison used paired player-within-year
bootstrap samples. All prespecified analyses were reported regardless of
direction. The generation-window amendment was made before outcome modeling
and documented as a response to differential data coverage.

## Limitations

First, Combine participants are already a highly selected group, so the results
do not describe all draft-eligible basketball players or the process by which
players receive Combine invitations. Second, U.S. Social Security and Census
records measure rarity relative to U.S. naming data. For international players,
the variables may measure unfamiliarity in the United States rather than rarity
within a culture of origin. Public names may also be nicknames or transliterated
forms.

Third, the conventional baseline was limited to variables measured consistently
across the 2000–2025 official Combine tables. It omitted production, prospect
rank, pathway, and age because complete-cohort coverage was not defensible.
Several athletic measurements were themselves substantially missing, and the
calibration results show that neither principal prediction model should be
treated as a production-quality draft probability model. The analysis asks
whether names add to this frozen baseline, not whether the baseline includes
all information used by NBA teams.

Fourth, the outcome was same-year draft selection, not an NBA appearance,
career value, media exposure, or fan recognition. Finally, the adjusted
association remains observational. Unmeasured geography, language, pathway,
school, representation, and visibility could influence both reference-dataset
name frequency and draft status.

## Future research

A direct test of memorability should use blinded raters who do not know player
outcomes and measure inter-rater agreement. Work on media attention could
predeclare outcomes such as article mentions or search interest. A broader
prospect denominator could test whether name variables relate to visibility or
Combine invitation before the final draft stage. Any culturally contextual
name model should use appropriate reference populations, avoid inferring
sensitive identity from names alone, and be evaluated for disparate errors.

External replication in another league or era would be more informative than
additional post hoc subgroup searches in this cohort. Secondary basketball
outcomes—such as appearing in an NBA game—should be studied only under a new
frozen protocol with a clearly defined follow-up window.

## Conclusion

Public-name distinctiveness was memorable as a hypothesis but not reliably
useful as a draft predictor. Within the NBA Combine population, adding given-
and surname-surprisal measures produced only a very small and uncertain change
in locked-period prediction, and neither co-primary adjusted association met
the multiplicity-corrected threshold. The strongest lesson is methodological:
preserving the right comparison group and confronting differential source
coverage mattered more than attaching a precise score to incomplete records.
